## Summary

The corpus covers the LDS Church's April 2026 federal lawsuit (Intellectual Reserve, Inc. v. Open Stories Foundation, D. Utah, Case No. 2:26-cv-00321) against John Dehlin and his "Mormon Stories" podcast/Open Stories Foundation for trademark and copyright infringement, plus the extensive public, legal, and media reaction to it. Court filings include the Church's complaint, Dehlin's answer/counterclaims and motion to dismiss, the Church's opposition to dismissal, amicus briefs from EFF and ACLU of Utah opposing the Church, and filings from a pro se movant (a self-identified Indigenous/Midewiwin traditional chief) seeking to intervene on First Amendment/treaty grounds, which the Church opposed. News outlets (Sun-Times, Axios, Salt Lake Tribune, Deseret News, Fox 13, Baptist Press, Salon) report both sides' claims neutrally or with light framing. A large number of YouTube/podcast commentators, split roughly between pro-Dehlin/anti-Church and pro-Church/anti-Dehlin framings, offer legal and moral analysis, often disagreeing about the Church's motives, the strength of its trademark claims, and whether the timing and history point to pretext or legitimate brand protection. A separate large sub-corpus—chapters from Ronald Huggins's biography "Lighthouse: Jerald and Sandra Tanner, Despised and Beloved Critics of Mormonism," a 1999 reader-email compilation, and Tanner-authored/pro-Tanner articles—covers a historically distinct but analogically invoked 1999-2000 Church copyright lawsuit against the Tanners' Utah Lighthouse Ministry over the Church Handbook of Instructions, which several commentators cite as a precedent for viewing the Dehlin suit as intimidation litigation. Additional unrelated or tangentially related articles (on Mormon history, doctrine, other ex-Mormon controversies, and general-interest topics) appear in the corpus without direct bearing on the lawsuit's merits.

## Positions

### Church's litigation is legitimate trademark/copyright enforcement about brand confusion, not about the word 'Mormon' or suppressing criticism

The Church argues (and various commentators/media pieces convey without endorsing) that its claims target only confusing branding, logos, colors, and copyrighted images that make consumers believe Mormon Stories is Church-affiliated; Dehlin retains full rights to criticize the Church and to use 'Mormon' generally, but not as a source-identifying mark alongside confusingly similar Church trademarks.

### Lawsuit is pretextual — a resource-driven attempt to silence/bankrupt a prominent critic

Dehlin, allies, and many commentators argue the Church's stated confusion rationale is implausible given 20+ years of tolerance, the Church's own abandonment of 'Mormon' since 2018, weak evidence of actual confusion, and the timing (after a child-abuse scandal exposure and amid membership decline); the real goal is to financially exhaust and reputationally damage Dehlin and chill other critics.

### No entity can own the word 'Mormon' — it is a generic/public term

Because 'Mormon' has been used for ~200 years by hundreds of denominations, sects, and individuals unaffiliated with the LDS Church (analogous to 'Catholic' or 'Christian'), and because the Church itself publicly abandoned the term as a brand identifier in 2018, no single entity — including the Church — can claim exclusive trademark rights over the bare word.

*Held by:* \[[19](https://chicago.suntimes.com/the-watchdogs/2026/06/29/church-of-jesus-christ-of-latter-day-saints-mormon-stories-john-dehlin-d-todd-christofferson-dallin-oaks)\], \[[21](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.42.0.pdf)\], \[[24](https://www.sltrib.com/religion/2026/06/22/mormon-stories-podcaster-fights)\], \[[35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2RDqcJR3mxs)\], \[[44](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.65.1.pdf)\], \[[45](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.66.1_1.pdf)\], \[[46](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.70.0.pdf)\], \[[47](https://www.abc4.com/news/religion/podcaster-responds-lds-church-trademark-infringement)\], \[[48](https://www.washingtonpost.com/religion/2026/06/23/latter-day-saints-critic-fights-use-mormon-title-popular-podcast)\], \[[49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wYp7gLEAQi0)\], \[[50](https://www.abc4.com/news/wasatch-front/organizations-file-in-support-of-mormon-stories-podcast-in-trademark-infringement-lawsuit-from-lds-church)\], \[[51](https://www.axios.com/local/salt-lake-city/2026/04/20/mormon-stories-church-trademark-lawsuit)\]

### The lawsuit does have legitimate merit and is not merely about the word 'Mormon' but genuine 'family of marks'/design confusion

Legal-minded defenders (including a retired IP attorney) argue the case is narrowly about stylized trademarks — logos, light-rays design, colors, Christus imagery — not the generic word, that the Church seeks only a modest disclaimer rather than damages or content control, and that Dehlin's own on-air admissions of intent to attract confused listeners support the Church's claim.

### Balanced/mixed fault — both sides bear some responsibility

Some observers conclude Mormon Stories did genuinely mimic Church branding and use copyrighted images improperly (deserving criticism) but also made substantial good-faith compliance efforts that the Church's public statements misleadingly downplay; the Church may be uneven in trademark enforcement and partly motivated by a desire to curb Dehlin's influence, while the suit is nonetheless likely to succeed given the Church's resources.

### Legally weak Church case, likely to be dismissed / fair use and laches favor defendants

Attorneys and amici argue the Rogers v. Grimaldi First Amendment test should govern, that 'Mormon' is likely generic and long in descriptive/nominative use, that laches bars claims given 20 years of Church knowledge/inaction, and that the Church's own 2018 abandonment and USPTO misrepresentations undercut its trademark rights, making dismissal likely.

### Analogous historical case — the 1999-2000 Church lawsuit against the Tanners was unjust intimidation litigation

A large sub-corpus (Huggins biography chapters, Tanner-authored articles, contemporary reader letters) argues that the Church's earlier copyright suit against Jerald and Sandra Tanner over the Church Handbook of Instructions was similarly disproportionate, selectively enforced (no suits against other distributors), driven by intent to intimidate/silence critics rather than protect genuine copyright interests, and ultimately settled on terms favorable to the Tanners with the injunction vacated.

## Cruxes of disagreement

### Has the Church abandoned trademark rights to 'Mormon' by publicly disavowing the term since 2018?

- **Abandonment argument (Dehlin/defendants/amici):** The Church 'publicly and expressly abandoned' use of 'Mormon' as a brand identifier in 2018 per President Nelson's directive, rebranded the Mormon Tabernacle Choir, purged the term from marketing, and called its use 'a major victory for Satan' — actions that under trademark law constitute abandonment through non-use with intent not to resume, especially since it then allegedly submitted false continued-use declarations to the USPTO.
- **No abandonment (Church):** The Church denies abandonment, stating it continuously used MORMON-formative marks in commerce for nearly 200 years and that Nelson's guidance to use the Church's correct name did not constitute abandonment of its intellectual property rights; it still holds and enforces registered trademarks including 'Book of Mormon Stories.'

> Instead, in 2018, the Church publicly and expressly abandoned (to the extent it owned any rights in “Mormon,” which Defendants do not concede) the very word “Mormon” that it now claims ownership and control of and sues to protect.
> — [Microsoft Word - Open Stories-Dehlin Answer and Counterclaims 4923-3123-9077 v.5.docx](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.42.0.pdf)

> the Church admits that Russell M. Nelson provided guidance encouraging use of the correct name of the Church but denies that such guidance constituted abandonment of the Church’s trademark rights
> — [18317411176](https://ecf.utd.uscourts.gov/doc1/18317411176)

*What would resolve it:* A judicial ruling on the abandonment defense/counterclaim, including findings on whether the Church's USPTO continued-use declarations (e.g., for MORMON TABERNACLE CHOIR, MORMON CHANNEL) were accurate or fraudulent.

### Does the Church's asserted evidence show actual, legally cognizable consumer confusion about Mormon Stories' affiliation?

- **Church/supporters:** The complaint cites numerous social-media comments and a 2022 episode in which a guest and Dehlin himself acknowledged that some listeners believed the podcast was Church-affiliated, which the Church argues constitutes actionable confusion under the Lanham Act's 'cardinal sin' standard.
- **Defendants/critics:** Critics note only a small fraction of cited comments actually originated on Mormon Stories' own pages (6 of 33/15), many were gathered after the lawsuit became public knowledge, confusion was described by commenters themselves as momentary, and no consumer confused actual podcast content (strongly critical) with official Church material.

> Mormon Stories’ unauthorized use of Defendants’ Marks, the Christus Symbol, the Light-Rays Design Marks, and other indicia has caused actual confusion in the marketplace, as illustrated by the following comments on the Mormon Stories Facebook and YouTube pages
> — [Case 2:26-cv-00321-JCB: INTELLECTUAL RESERVE, INC. v. OPEN STORIES FOUNDATION](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.1.0.pdf)

> In truth, only 6 comments in paragraph 54 of the Complaint were posted on the MORMON STORIES Facebook or YouTube pages.
> — [Microsoft Word - Open Stories-Dehlin Answer and Counterclaims 4923-3123-9077 v.5.docx](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.42.0.pdf)

*What would resolve it:* Discovery/expert survey evidence on actual consumer confusion rates and a court ruling on whether the cited comments meet the 'substantial number of consumers' standard.

### Is 'Mormon' a generic term incapable of exclusive trademark protection, or a valid, enforceable source identifier?

- **Generic-term view (EFF, ACLU, defendants):** 'Mormon' functions like 'Catholic' — describing an entire ethnoreligious culture spanning 400+ sects, most unaffiliated with the LDS Church — and the USPTO itself denied the Church's 2002 application to broadly trademark 'Mormon' on genericness grounds.
- **Valid mark view (Church):** The Church holds valid, in some cases incontestable, federal trademark registrations incorporating MORMON (e.g., Book of Mormon Stories, Mormon Tabernacle Choir, Mormon Channel) constituting a 'family of marks' continuously used in commerce for nearly 200 years, and the Lanham Act does not categorically bar protection for terms with historical/religious significance.

> “Mormons” are members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (the “LDS Church”), as well as other faiths/denominations/groups centered on the Book of Mormon, just as “Catholic” is a commonly understood term for members of the Catholic Church.
> — [gov.uscourts.utd.163775.66.1_1](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.66.1_1.pdf)

> The Lanham Act does not categorically limit trademark rights in or preclude registration of terms with such significance.
> — [gov.uscourts.utd.163775.26.0](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.26.0.pdf)

*What would resolve it:* A court determination of genericness/distinctiveness for each asserted MORMON mark, informed by the 2005 USPTO denial precedent and evidence of public usage patterns.

### Should the Rogers v. Grimaldi First Amendment test or the ordinary Lanham Act likelihood-of-confusion test govern this case?

- **Rogers-test proponents (EFF, defendants):** Because MORMON STORIES is the title of an expressive work (a podcast) rather than a commercial product mark, Rogers should apply, asking only whether the use is artistically relevant and explicitly misleading — a standard that would likely result in early dismissal and protect speech from costly discovery.
- **Standard-confusion-test proponents (Church):** Under Jack Daniel's v. VIP Products, Rogers does not apply when a mark is used 'as a mark' / source identifier for one's own goods/services (here, the podcast brand across hundreds of episodes), so the full likelihood-of-confusion analysis governs and cannot be resolved at the pleading stage.

> The Supreme Court held that First Amendment protections under Rogers do not shield a defendant from the standard likelihood of confusion test under the Lanham Act when a defendant uses a mark as a source identifier for the commercial sale of goods.
> — [Pleading Template](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.41.0_1.pdf)

> the Supreme Court made clear that the Rogers test has no application “when an alleged infringer uses a trademark . . . as a designation of source for
> — [gov.uscourts.utd.163775.71.0](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.71.0.pdf)

> Rogers asks two comparatively straightforward questions: (1) whether the use is artistically relevant to the expressive work, and (2) whether it is explicitly misleading
> — [gov.uscourts.utd.163775.66.1_1](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.66.1_1.pdf)

*What would resolve it:* The court's ruling on the motion to dismiss regarding which legal test applies to a podcast title/branding used across a commercial-adjacent nonprofit.

### Does laches bar the Church's claims given ~20 years of alleged inaction?

- **Laches applies (defendants):** Dehlin used MORMON STORIES openly and continuously since 2005 with the Church's constructive or actual knowledge (including a 2015 press release referencing his podcast), and the Church's 20-year delay caused severe prejudice through Defendants' growth and investment, barring the claims.
- **Laches does not apply / is fact-intensive (Church):** Laches is a fact-intensive affirmative defense generally unsuited to resolution at the motion-to-dismiss stage; it does not bar injunctive relief (the only remedy sought on trademark claims); does not apply to willful infringement; and the infringement (via the 2022 blue light-rays rebrand and Christus imagery) became progressively more egregious only in recent years, resetting the relevant delay period.

> Defendants’ widespread, open use of MORMON STORIES for decades leaves no room for an inference other than the Church having knowledge of the accused actions—or at a minimum constructive knowledge—long before it filed this case, thus triggering a presumption of laches
> — [Pleading Template](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.41.0_1.pdf)

> Laches is a fact-intensive affirmative defense that generally cannot be resolved on a motion to dismiss.
> — [gov.uscourts.utd.163775.71.0](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.71.0.pdf)

> laches “does not apply . . . in cases of willful infringement.”
> — [gov.uscourts.utd.163775.71.0](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.utd.163775/gov.uscourts.utd.163775.71.0.pdf)

*What would resolve it:* The court's ruling on the motion to dismiss/summary judgment addressing when the relevant infringing conduct began and whether prejudice and inexcusable delay are established.

### What did the Church actually demand in mediation, and was the demand reasonable?

- **Dehlin's account:** The Church's public statements misleadingly minimized its demands as merely 'a brief disclaimer,' when in mediation it allegedly demanded he drop 'Mormon' from his name entirely (e.g., 'Ex-Mormon Stories with Dr. John Dehlin'), agree never to use 'Mormon' in any future project, never attempt to trademark his own names, concede the Church owns all rights to 'Mormon,' and read an audible disclaimer repeated every 10-20 minutes throughout every episode indefinitely — demands Dehlin calls unreasonable and coercive given the threat of a multimillion-dollar lawsuit.
- **Church's account / sympathetic commentators:** The Church's filings and press statements describe the ask as simple: a brief disclaimer (verbal or written) at the start of episodes stating non-affiliation, alongside logo/branding changes already substantially made; commentators sympathetic to the Church see this as a modest, standard request and view Dehlin's refusal (specifically of a beginning-of-episode audible disclaimer) as the actual sticking point, not a broader demand to abandon 'Mormon' altogether.

> the Church proposed a simple solution: a brief disclaimer that the podcast is not affiliated with or endorsed by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.
> — [Getting It Right: Clarifying Trademark and Branding Concerns](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/getting-it-right-clarifying-trademark-branding-concerns)

> defendants refused to include a simple disclaimer at the beginning of podcast episodes, either verbally or in writing, acknowledge that Mormon Stories is not affiliated with the church.
> — [The "Unreasonable" Demand That Got Mormon Stories Sued](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3x5EXeZ2_Ig)

*What would resolve it:* Discovery of mediation communications/settlement offers (currently non-public) that would establish the actual scope of the Church's demands.

### Why did the Church sue now, after ~20 years of apparent tolerance?

- **Pretext/timing-suspicious view:** Commentators and at least one news outlet note the suit followed closely after Mormon Stories helped expose a child sex abuse cover-up implicating a leader's brother (D. Todd Christofferson), amid unprecedented U.S. membership decline, and shortly after President Nelson's death — suggesting retaliatory or reputation-management motives rather than a sudden trademark concern.
- **Legitimate escalation view:** The Church and defenders argue the timing reflects the branding dispute becoming acute only after Mormon Stories' December 2022 rebrand to blue/light-rays/Christus imagery increasingly mirroring official Church branding, with confusion cited as 'increasingly more blatant over time,' plus a trademark's use-it-or-lose-it renewal timeline cited by some commentators.

> the unusual legal assault comes roughly two months after “Mormon Stories” hosted Edward Nachel, a former Chicago area resident who revealed he’d been part of the excommunication process of former McHenry County church leader Wade Christofferson
> — [Latter-day Saints sues podcaster who exposed alleged child sex abuse by ex-Mormon leader in Chicago region - Chicago Sun-Times](https://chicago.suntimes.com/the-watchdogs/2026/04/29/latter-day-saints-sues-podcaster-mormon-stories-john-dehlin-todd-wade-christofferson)

> The Church intends to prove that Defendants’ infringement became increasingly more blatant over time until the Church had no choice but to take action to protect its intellectual property.
